Rotation moves in the disassembler

With rotation moves on, the disassembler may turn a group of pieces by 90° as one step, alongside the usual straight slides. This page shows which turns are covered and how BurrTools proves that a turn is physically possible. The animations below run a port of the solver's own geometry code, so every verdict you see is computed, not drawn.

What a rotation move is

A rotation move turns one group of pieces a quarter turn (±90°) about an axis parallel to X, Y or Z. All other pieces stay where they are. The axis passes through a cube centre or a cube corner, because only those two kinds of pivot map the grid onto itself after a quarter turn. While the group turns, it may also shift a little sideways, in the plane of the turn. That gives three kinds of path:

Pure turn 1 path

A plain quarter turn about the pivot. The large majority of moves need nothing more.

Wiggle 8 directions × 4 amounts

Turns while drifting along one compass direction by a·b(θ), where b(θ) = (cos θ + sin θ − 1)/(√2 − 1) is the bulge of a turning cube. The drift is 0 at both ends and largest at 45°. Stored as a 16-segment polyline, and that polyline is the motion that gets checked.

Slide 8 directions × 4 amounts

Shifts by a, turns about the shifted pivot, then shifts back. Three straight segments.

The animations exaggerate the shift to 0.4 cells; the dashed curve traces the group's centre. The default amounts are 0.1, 0.2, 0.3 and 0.4 cells.

Covered

  • Any group of whole pieces turning together, up to 32 pieces per (sub)problem.
  • All three axes, both senses (+90° and −90°).
  • Pivots at cube centres and cube corners, inside the turning group's footprint or up to half a cell beyond it.
  • 65 paths per sense: the pure turn, then for each amount 0.1 … 0.4 the 8 wiggles and the 8 slides. The search tries them in this order and records the first clear one.
  • Cube grids only. Tables are sized from the puzzle's extents.

Not covered (yet)

  • Half turns as one move. A 180° turn is found only if it splits into two quarter turns with a free pose in between.
  • Moving along the axis during the turn.
  • Arbitrary "shimmy" paths outside the 65-path family. A configuration-space planner is planned (phase 3).
  • Pivots on edge midpoints: a quarter turn about them leaves cubes off the grid.
  • Groups wider than the tables. These are skipped, never guessed, and the result says "turns may be missing".

From 3D to flat layers

A turn about an axis-parallel line, combined with a shift perpendicular to that line, keeps every cube in its own layer along the axis. Cubes in different layers can at most touch across a face plane. They can never overlap. So the 3D question splits exactly into independent 2D questions, one per layer: do the moving squares of this layer stay clear of the static squares of the same layer? This is not an approximation. Static cubes in layers without moving cubes are dropped up front.

Exploded along the Z axis: each layer is checked on its own. Blue cubes turn, grey cubes stand still. In the code, X maps to the plane (y, z), Y to (z, x) and Z to (x, y), so a positive angle is right-handed about every axis.

The physical cube

Real pieces are a little undersized and their edges are cut, which is exactly what makes some turns possible. The model has explicit parameters, reported with every result instead of hidden in constants:

  • Play g: each cube has side 1 − g and sits centred in its cell, leaving a gap g between neighbours. It must be greater than 0. Without play, a cube turning about a corner it shares with a diagonal neighbour touches it for the whole turn, and no finite check can prove that clear.
  • 45° bevel k: the edges parallel to the axis are cut off at 45°, removing k from each adjacent face, so the bevel face is k·√2 wide. This matches 3D-printed pieces: puzzlecad's $burr_bevel is the width of that face (each face is set back by bevel/√2), so k = $burr_bevel / (√2 · $burr_scale) and g = 2 · $burr_inset / $burr_scale.
  • Rounding r: optionally, a rounding radius on the same edges. It defaults to 0 and is there for pieces that really are rounded. Printed pieces use the bevel.

The default is play 0.081 with sharp edges, the model PR #50's code assumed implicitly, until there are physically verified moves to calibrate against. The model is slightly optimistic in one respect: every cube edge gets beveled, including edges that puzzlecad leaves flat (between cubes of one piece, and concave edges).

Spinning beside a face neighbour

A single cube turns next to a static neighbour. As a pure turn about its own centre, sharp corners hit the neighbour. The bevel has to grow until the farthest point of the cube, a bevel end at distance √(h² + (h−k)²), stays inside the neighbour's face at 1 − h (with h = (1 − g)/2). As a bulge wiggle away from the neighbour, the cube's corner glides along the neighbour's face instead. That works even with sharp edges, once the shift reaches the cube's own bulge minus the play, h(√2 − 1) − g.

Why the wiggle has this shape

A tight turn keeps the turning piece in contact with a static one the whole time; the play only separates them at the start and at the end. Take a cube of half side h turning about its centre. Its extent along an axis is h·(cos θ + sin θ), so its leading face advances by h·(√2 − 1)·b(θ). A cube touching a corner pivot advances by the same shape, scaled by 0.5 + h. If the wiggle shifts the group away by exactly that amount, the cube's corner glides along the plane its face started on, and it never pushes into the neighbour.

PR #50 used sin 2θ, which approximates b to within 0.043. But it lags b everywhere except at 0°, 45° and 90°, and the lag is largest early and late in the turn, where the play is tightest. With almost no play, as on printed pieces, that lag becomes a collision.

Proving a path clear

Every path is a polyline in configuration space (θ, du, dv). A moving point p goes to R(θ)(p − P) + P + d, where P is the pivot. The checker decides each segment with one measurement: the clearance, the signed distance between the closest pair of same-layer (moving, static) cubes. When two convex polygons are apart, that is their distance (the minimum vertex-to-edge distance). When they overlap, it is minus the penetration depth (the smallest overlap over all edge normals, by the separating axis test). Rounding subtracts 2r.

Within a segment, no point of the group moves more than

L = |Δd| + ρ · |Δθ| ρ = largest distance of a moving cube corner from the pivot

Distance between sets changes by at most as much as the points move. So if the clearance at the middle of an interval of width 2δ is at least L·δ, the group cannot reach a static cube anywhere in that interval: the interval is proven clear. A negative clearance at a sample proves a collision. Anything else gets split in half and checked again. A segment still undecided once the motion it covers drops below 10⁻⁷ cells is treated as blocked. The result is sound: no tunnelling through corners, and no step-count constant.

Lookup tables: no geometry at solve time

Along a fixed path, each (moving cube, static cube) pair either collides or doesn't, regardless of every other cube. A path is blocked exactly when some moving cube collides with some static cube of its layer. So for each path, BurrTools precomputes a table: for every offset of a moving cube from the pivot, the list of static offsets that would block it. The table is filled by the certified checker, so it is exactly as precise as the checker. At solve time, a turn costs a few dozen lookups per cube, with no trigonometry.

Only the +90° tables for the pure turn and for directions (1,0) and (1,1) are computed with the checker. The other six directions are turned copies, since turning the whole scene about the pivot commutes with the motion, and every −90° table is a mirrored copy. Tests compare these derived tables against directly computed ones. Offsets are doubled so they stay integers: both even for a pivot at a cube centre, both odd for one at a corner.

How the solver finds turns

Rotation moves are a third phase in movementAnalysator_c::find, after the translation moves. It runs only when the tables exist, so with the option off the cost is one null check per search node. For every node:

  1. For each axis and sense

    Project every piece onto the plane of the turn, and fetch from a shared cache the pivots at which piece j blocks piece i on every path. That depends only on the two pieces' shapes, orientations and relative position, so all worker threads share one cache.

  2. For each pivot

    Centre and corner pivots in the pieces' footprint (mixed parity is skipped). Pieces with cubes beyond the table radius cannot turn there; the context notes the skip.

  3. Groups by closure

    If j blocks i on every path, they must turn together. The candidate groups are the unions of closures of this relation, Cutler-style, instead of all 2ⁿ subsets. A test compares the result against exhaustive enumeration.

  4. End pose

    Closure already guarantees that the end cells are free. The turn must also change something, since turning a piece onto its own cells is not a move.

  5. First clear path

    firstClear walks the 65 tables against one occupancy bitmap of the static pieces. A precomputed blocked-on-every-path set rejects most candidates before any table walk.

  6. Canonical state

    Piece 0 is the reference frame and never moves. A group containing it is stored as the complement turning instead: the other sense for a pure turn, or the same path marked inverse for wiggles and slides. Symmetric pieces are normalised to their smallest orientation with the same cells, so the same physical state is not visited twice.

The path family is closed under reversal: a +90° wiggle run backwards is a −90° wiggle in the same direction, and the same holds for slides. So every rotation edge in the search graph is undirected, which the three-front breadth-first search relies on. A move records the path index, and the GUI animates poseAt(makePath(spec), t), the same function the checker sampled. What you see is what was checked.

Math review while writing this page. I re-derived the code's geometry and found it correct:
  • the Lipschitz bound;
  • the separating-axis normals of the turned octagon;
  • the hit range used to fill the tables, √2·(1−g) + shift;
  • the reversal closure of the path family;
  • the conversion from puzzlecad, checked against puzzlecad's source.
The one error was in the design doc: it claimed the face-neighbour spin needs rounding r ≥ (√2−1)/2 ≈ 0.207. That is wrong at any play; it is 0.264 at play 0.081 and 0.5 at play 0. The code's test already used the correct formula. The doc is fixed in commit f5b8a74e.

Open questions

  • Default play and bevel: these need moves verified on physical copies (Clutch 4 and others) to calibrate against.
  • With turns allowed, the breadth-first search finds shorter disassemblies that use them once pieces are partly out (PelikanBurr: 3 turns). Should turns rank equal to slides, or count only when translations fail?
  • Should half turns be single moves? Should results report the minimum play a move needs?
  • Cost: building the default tables takes about 1.8 s for PelikanBurr (dev build). DraculasDentalDesaster with -R takes 5.5 s wall, against 0.05 s without turns.
